# Defining the problem before writing

Part of Minto's work is about solving problems, not just writing. Her point: many proposals and reports come out muddled because the problem was never defined precisely. If the definition is clear, the document's introduction and key line almost write themselves.

## The three questions

1. **What's going on?**
   - **Starting scene:** the structure or process that existed and was working (a way of selling, a system, a team, a distribution chain). Describe it simply.
   - **Disturbing event:** what happened, or what is likely to happen, that upsets that scene. It can be:
     - *External:* a new competitor, a change in technology, a regulation or policy from the government or from customers.
     - *Internal:* the company added a process, installed a system, entered another market, changed its product line.
     - *Newly recognized:* results below expectations, a product falling behind, research suggesting a change in customers.
     If there's no information about the event (common in proposals), don't invent it: go straight to R1.

2. **What don't we like? (R1, undesired result)**
   What the scene produces now that the reader doesn't want: losing share, falling sales, eroding margin, an expected opportunity that won't materialize. There can be more than one R1. Write it as briefly as possible. Sometimes R1 is an opportunity that isn't being seized.

3. **What do we want instead? (R2, desired result)**
   What the reader wants the scene to produce. Write it in terms of an end product, with a number or a concrete state, so you know when it's been achieved and can choose between solutions:
   - Hit the year-end growth target.
   - Cut launch time to a third.
   - Have the system work as designed.

The **problem is the gap between R1 and R2.** The causes usually lie in the starting scene, and the solution usually consists of changing something in that structure or process.

## From the definition to the document's question

The question depends on where the reader is in the story:

| Story | Complication | Question |
|---|---|---|
| There's a problem and no solution | R1 versus R2 | What do we do? |
| There's a problem and a solution has already been chosen | That solution exists | Is it the right one? or How do we implement it? |
| A solution was applied and didn't work | The solution doesn't deliver the result | What do we do now? |

Problems can have several layers (a second solution that didn't work either). In that case, the situation summarizes the earlier layers in a few lines and the complication is only the latest one.

## Card to deliver

```
Starting scene:       ...
Disturbing event:     ... (or "no information")
R1 (undesired):       ...
R2 (desired):         ... (with a number or concrete state)
Gap:                  R1 versus R2 in one line
Question:             ...
SCQ introduction:     S: ... | C: ... | Q: ...
```

## Ordering the analysis

Once the problem is defined, Minto proposes a sequence of analysis:

1. Is there a problem?
2. Where is it?
3. Why does it exist?
4. What could we do?
5. What should we do?

For steps 2 and 3, **diagnostic frameworks** help: a drawing of the structure or process where the problem occurs, built in one of three ways (dividing into parts, following cause and effect, or classifying). That drawing reveals the concrete yes-or-no questions you need to answer to find the causes. It's best to order them from easiest to hardest to rule out.

For steps 4 and 5, **logic trees** help: lay out every possible action to move each cause and estimate the impact of each one. A logic tree also works for reviewing a finished document: if the groups of ideas don't hang neatly from a tree, there's a gap or an overlap.

## Example

A content agency gets this request from a client: "We need more leads from the blog".

```
Starting scene:       The blog publishes 8 articles a month and is the main source of organic leads.
Disturbing event:     Since March, search engines show AI-generated answers above the results.
R1:                   Organic traffic dropped and blog leads went down.
R2:                   [missing: monthly blog lead target]
Gap:                  Current leads versus the target.
Question:             What do we change in the blog to recover the leads?
```

With this card, the proposal is no longer "more articles". The question points to the starting scene (what gets published and how it's distributed), and the missing data (R2) is requested before proposing anything.
